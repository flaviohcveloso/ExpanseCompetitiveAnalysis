NVIDIA Run:ai

A policy-driven GPU orchestrator that dynamically allocates fractional GPUs across an enterprise's AI workloads — now part of NVIDIA's software stack.

What it does

Run:ai sits on top of Kubernetes and takes over the decision of how GPUs get handed out. Its core mechanic is fractional GPU allocation: instead of giving a whole GPU to one workload, it can split a GPU across several inference, embedding and generation jobs running concurrently, matching resources to demand in real time. On top of that sits what NVIDIA calls an "AI-native workload orchestration engine" — it pools GPUs across public cloud, private cloud, hybrid and on-prem environments and treats them as one scalable resource, sometimes described as an "AI factory."

The other half of the product is governance: a policy engine that gives IT centralized control over who gets priority access to GPUs, by department, project or team, with the goal of eliminating the manual, ad-hoc allocation that causes both idle capacity and noisy-neighbor contention.

Run:ai was acquired by NVIDIA and is now sold and developed as part of NVIDIA's enterprise software stack — which is also why its roadmap and hardware assumptions increasingly lean toward NVIDIA GPUs specifically, rather than staying silicon-agnostic.

Where it overlaps with Expanse

Both products exist to close the gap between GPUs an organization owns and GPUs it actually gets useful work out of, and both talk directly to Kubernetes clusters. A team evaluating Expanse will very plausibly have Run:ai (or a Slurm-based equivalent) already sitting underneath it as the scheduler.

Where it differs

Run:ai is the scheduler — it makes the real-time placement and allocation decision itself, after a job has already been submitted, using fractional GPU tricks and policy rules. Expanse doesn't try to replace that decision-maker; it sits one step earlier, predicting — before a job is even submitted — how much GPU, memory and time it will actually need, and whether it's likely to fail. In practice: Run:ai changes how a job gets placed once you've asked for resources; Expanse changes what you ask for in the first place, and can feed that better number into Run:ai, Slurm or Nomad rather than competing with any of them. It's also worth noting that Run:ai's tightening integration with NVIDIA's stack is a trade-off some multi-vendor or on-prem-first teams weigh carefully, whereas Expanse's telemetry-first approach doesn't require standardizing on one hardware vendor.

What it does

ClearML describes itself as "the Infrastructure Platform for AI Builders," and the product footprint backs that up. Its Infrastructure Control Plane is a single-pane-of-glass for GPU allocation, monitoring and orchestration across Kubernetes, Slurm, PBS and bare metal, explicitly built to be silicon-agnostic across NVIDIA, AMD, Arm and Intel hardware. It includes a built-in job scheduler, quota management with hierarchical allocation policies, over-quota limits, and fractional GPU support the company says can increase workload capacity "10X."

That sits alongside two other products: an AI Development Center (an IDE-style environment with data integration, pipelines, model repository and CI/CD) and a GenAI App Engine for deploying LLM-based applications. Deployment covers on-prem, air-gapped, hybrid and multi-cloud, and the platform adds enterprise features — multi-tenant RBAC, SSO/LDAP, a billing API for chargeback — on top of what it frames as "a better Kubernetes experience for AI workloads."

In other words: ClearML isn't a single tool, it's a platform decision. Adopting it typically means routing scheduling, quota policy and a meaningful share of ML tooling through ClearML itself.

Where it overlaps with Expanse

This is the closest overlap in supported environments: ClearML explicitly supports Slurm alongside Kubernetes and bare metal, matching Expanse's own HPC-first footprint. Both also frame the problem the same way — GPU clusters sitting well below full utilization — and both pitch deployment on infrastructure the customer already owns, without a cloud migration.

Where it differs

ClearML is broad where Expanse is narrow. ClearML's control plane is a scheduler and quota engine in its own right — teams adopt it to make allocation decisions, not just to get better numbers to feed an existing one — and it extends well past infrastructure into experiment tracking, pipelines and GenAI app deployment. Expanse doesn't attempt any of that: it doesn't schedule, doesn't track experiments, and doesn't deploy models. Its entire surface area is the prediction — resource fit and failure risk — that happens in the seconds before a job is submitted to whatever scheduler you already run, ClearML's or otherwise. The practical question for a buyer is really scope: a full platform migration (ClearML) versus a single, narrow layer that installs without changing the rest of the stack (Expanse).

What it does

CAST AI's GPU product, branded OMNI Compute for AI, pitches itself as a way to "deploy more AI workloads on fewer GPUs anywhere." It consolidates GPU capacity across multiple cloud providers and regions into a single Kubernetes cluster, then optimizes what's already running on it: time-slicing to share one GPU across up to 48 workload replicas, MIG partitioning to split A100/A30/H100 GPUs into physically isolated instances, GPU-aware bin-packing, and automated fallback between spot and on-demand capacity.

Everything is Kubernetes-native — built on ResourceClaims, deployed across AWS EKS, Google GKE, Azure AKS and Oracle Cloud — with real-time cost attribution per workload and team, which is the point: CAST AI is sold and measured as a cost tool, not a research-infrastructure tool.

Notably, CAST AI's own marketing leans on utilization numbers to make its case — including an industry claim that average GPU utilization across Kubernetes clusters sits around 5%, with Azure as low as 2%.

Where it overlaps with Expanse

Both companies point at the same underlying evidence — GPU clusters running at a small fraction of their real capacity — and both frame the fix as software, not more hardware. Both also emphasize automated setup with minimal engineering lift.

Where it differs

CAST AI's reach stops at Kubernetes on public cloud — there's no on-prem, no bare metal, no Slurm or Nomad, which rules out most of the research labs, hedge funds and neoclouds Expanse is built for. Its optimization also happens after the fact: workloads are already running, and CAST AI repacks, shares or migrates them to cut cost. Expanse's prediction happens before submission, on whatever scheduler the job would have gone to anyway, and isn't priced against cloud spend the way a FinOps tool is. The two could plausibly coexist for a cloud-heavy team — CAST AI optimizing the public-cloud portion of the fleet, Expanse covering the owned-cluster portion CAST AI doesn't reach at all.
